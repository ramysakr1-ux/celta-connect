# Admissions, staffing and close-out — August 2026

Everything decided in the session of 8–9 August. Written as build rules, not as description.

---

## §1 Applying

**The applicant journey is four steps, not three.** Apply → task in Connect → interview → the reply. The interview is a stage, not a gap between two others: a place is decided in the conversation, and everything before it only decides whether the conversation is worth having.

**The application page is public and branded.** Linked from the centre's own website. No account, no password. The applicant picks which intake they want. Source is recorded (website, referral, Instagram) — the only honest measure of which channel is worth paying for.

**The task opens in Connect, not by email.** Cambridge's language awareness questions plus an extended writing task where the applicant chooses one of the centre's three or four prompts. It saves as they type; the email only tells them it is waiting. Both parts are required before anyone can be accepted (Handbook 7.2).

**Five acknowledgements are ticked before the form can be submitted**, one per section of the course commitments document:
1. Full-time and intensive; should not be working during it.
2. Observed teaching from the first week, feedback in front of the group.
3. A laptop, and reliable internet if any part is online.
4. Evening and weekend work.
5. The code of conduct — respect, care with volunteer learners, honest work including AI, confidentiality.

**A sixth, separate consent — resolved 16 Aug, applies to every applicant, not just rejected ones.** At the point of applying (before interview, before any outcome is known), one line: whether accepted or rejected, their application file is kept at the centre. Not folded into the five course-commitments acknowledgements above (those are signed only once accepted) — this one is checked by everyone who applies, since it covers the case that matters most: someone who's rejected and never signs anything else.

Accepted **once**, at application. Nothing further is signed. The file keeps the name, the timestamp, and **a stored copy of the document text as it stood that day** — not a version number. An assessor asking what a candidate agreed to must be shown the actual words.

### No enquiry stage

Dropped. The pipeline is five stages: applied, interviewed, offered, deposit paid, paid in full. A manual enquiry count only reflects how diligent an administrator was that month, and the drop that costs money is offer-to-deposit, which is fully covered. If channel data at the top of the funnel is ever wanted, it comes from a short question form on the website that creates the enquiry automatically — never from typing.

## §2 Reading the task, and booking the interview

**Three lanes, and the app never writes a rejection.**

| Reading | What happens |
|---|---|
| Clear on every criterion | Invitation drafted, held, sent with interview times |
| Mixed or borderline | Queued for a human. Nothing sent. |
| Clear problems | A tutor is notified. Nothing sent. The tutor reads it themselves. |

Not at any confidence, not with any setting, does the app send a rejection. A rejection is a named person's words, because a candidate turned down can ask why.

**A fifteen-minute hold between the verdict and the email.** Two jobs: an invitation landing eleven seconds after submission tells the applicant a machine decided; and the admin sees the pending invitation with a **Hold** button, so anything caught in that window never reaches the applicant. The hold is recorded with who pressed it.

**A held invitation can still be sent by hand.** An admin flags it, a tutor reads the task, disagrees, and sends the same email themselves. One template, two ways out, only ever one send — and the file records which it was.

**Shadow mode first.** From the first application the reading is written on every task and nothing is sent. Tutors mark and decide as usual. After two cohorts the centre has every verdict against every human decision, and the threshold for auto-booking is set on evidence. It also answers the assessor: the reading informed an invitation to interview, never a place, an offer or a rejection.

### Interview availability

**Slots are generated from a weekly pattern, not typed in.** Each interviewer sets days, hours and mode once; the app produces bookable slots three weeks ahead and stops the moment somebody blocks time out.

- Availability is the **centre's**, not the course timetable's — an interviewer is rarely teaching the course they are recruiting for.
- 45-minute interviews plus a ten-minute gap. 24-hour cut-off, so an interviewer has the evening to read the task.
- Mode is per interviewer (in person, online, either) and the applicant is told which before choosing.
- **More than one interviewer can be free at once.** The applicant sees a time, not a roster; it goes to whoever has interviewed least this intake, and an admin can override to a named person.
- **A panel books both interviewers as one slot** — a trainer in training sitting in, or a second opinion on a borderline applicant.
- Blocking a booked slot requires rebooking the applicant first, and they are emailed by a person.
- **When there is nothing to offer:** no slot is ever invented. The applicant gets an honest holding email ("we would like to meet you, we are finding a time, we will write within two working days") and admissions sees a flag that does not clear until slots exist.

### One booking, three announcements

The **interview log entry is the event**. Email (to admissions and the named interviewer, with a link to the marked task) and optional Slack/Teams are copies. If the copies fail, the booking still happened.

### Interview questions

**Seven fixed questions plus two drawn per applicant.**

The fixed set is the centre's own, imported at setup like assignment briefs. Everyone is asked the same ones — that is what makes a rejection defensible. Connect ships a starter set the centre edits, and the bank checks coverage against what the handbook asks selection to assess, saying **None** where an area has no question. A centre running face-to-face has no digital-literacy question until it sets up a mixed-mode course, at which point 7.2 requires one.

The drawn pair comes from the rows the reading **flagged**, weakest first. Suggested, never asked automatically; if nothing is flagged the slots stay empty. What the interviewer actually asked is what goes on the record. This is why the task is marked before the interview.

### The interview record

Carries the questions and the answers, and **two signatures typed at the end of the interview with both people still in the room**:
- **Interviewer** — required. 7.2 asks who conducted selection, not just when.
- **Applicant** — optional and worth having. Confirms the notes reflect the conversation, which matters if a rejection is challenged.

An online interview signs the same way.

## §3 The waiting list

**A waiting list is an interviewed list.** Only interviewed-and-suitable applicants can be offered a place. Anyone not yet interviewed appears on the list but is not selectable, and the row says why. Names collected on a form are a mailing list.

**Every person carries which course or courses they are waiting for**, with dates and mode. A summer course fills differently from an autumn one, and somebody waiting for July cannot be offered April. Someone on two lists appears on both; accepting one removes them from the other, and both lists say so.

**Ranked by suitability, then by date.** A tutor can move somebody up and the reason is recorded.

**The offer expires in 48 hours** and then goes to the next person automatically, with the first told plainly. Nobody holds two places at once.

**When the course starts they stay on the courses they chose** — not rolled onto whatever comes next. Moving a summer applicant to April is how a centre loses them. After two intakes with no place, the centre writes properly rather than going silent.

**Clicking a name opens the application file** — form, both marked tasks, interview record, selection outcome, commitments accepted. Nothing is re-collected when a place frees.

## §4 Fees and payment

**Connect never holds money and never decides a refund.**

Four methods, and a real course uses several: bank transfer (most common), cash, card (optional), employer or sponsor invoice.

**Card is an option, not the design.** The centre connects **their own** account with a provider — iyzico or PayTR for Turkish cards, Stripe/Mollie/Adyen elsewhere. Connect stores a reference and no card numbers. The Pay link in the acceptance email opens the provider's own page; the provider settles to the centre's bank; Connect is told it happened. Cost roughly 2–3%, plus a few days of KYC onboarding.

**The distinction that runs through the screen:** a card payment says **Confirmed** because the provider said so. Everything else says **Marked by** and a name. Same figure, different kind of fact.

**One currency per course**, set by the centre. A candidate paying from abroad is converted by their own bank.

**Instalments are a decision, not a default.** Turkish cards routinely split over 3/6/9 months, which turns the pipeline from paid/not-paid into on-schedule/behind. A missed instalment is a payments task, never an automatic consequence — nobody loses a place because a card expired.

**A waiver or discount records who agreed it and in what role**, whatever the method.

### Acceptance and the deposit

**Two routes to the workspace invitation, and the record says which was used.**

- **Automatic.** A payment or deposit that clears through a connected provider sends the invitation itself. Nobody waits on an administrator noticing.
- **Manual.** An administrator marks the place secured and sends it. Used when the centre has agreed terms and the money is coming later, which happens.

Either way the payment state stays visible, so a candidate on the course who has not paid is a flag on the payments screen rather than something nobody remembers.

Until the invitation is sent the applicant exists **only on the admin's admissions page** — no account, no Connect access. The invitation fires on the deposit being recorded, whether that is a card confirmation minutes later or an administrator marking a transfer days later.

## §5 Email

Nineteen emails, and nobody receives more than five. One candidate accepted, enrolled and passed gets: acknowledgement, interview invitation, offer, workspace invitation, starts-Monday. **Nothing during the five weeks** — everything in the course is an announcement in the app, anchored to the timetable, one a day at most.

**Every email is from the centre.** The sender is the centre's name, reply-to is a centre address. Connect never appears in anyone's inbox.

**Three reply routes, per email:**
- *Do not reply* — automated confirmations, task-waiting, workspace ready, staff invitations, internal notifications.
- *Replies to admissions* — the interview invitation (its body says "if none of these suit, reply"), the offer, all three waiting-list emails, the assessor pack.
- *Replies to the tutor who wrote it* — both rejections and both welcome emails. Somebody just turned down needs to answer the person who decided.

**Money and onboarding never share an email.** The offer carries the fee and the deadline and nothing else; the workspace invitation follows once the deposit clears.

**Three waiting-list emails, not one:** you are on the list (position and a date), a place has opened (48-hour clock), and the course filled before a place came free. The third is the one centres forget and the cheapest to send.

### Delivery records — no BCC

**A copy in your inbox proves the email left. It does not prove it arrived.** Four states reported by the mail provider: sent, delivered, opened, bounced.

- **Bounced is the only state that needs a person**, so it is the only one that produces a task — on the admissions screen, with the provider's reason in plain words ("no such domain", not a status code).
- The task does not clear itself. It stays until the email is delivered.
- After two failures Connect stops trying that address and asks for a new one.
- Correcting the address resends automatically; both attempts stay on the record.
- **Opened is shown for chasing and never treated as proof** — image blocking makes it unreliable in both directions.
- **Nobody is BCC'd on anything.** The delivery record answers the question BCC cannot.

The bounce that matters is the workspace invitation: a paid-up candidate with no way into their course.

## §6 The pre-course task

**Nothing is handed in.** Cambridge publishes the answer key, so the task is self-check. It saves as they type, and the key is released to the whole cohort at a point the centre sets — the Friday before, or with the day-one activity.

The only signal worth watching is whether they opened it. A candidate who has not opened it nine days out is the candidate who arrives cold.

## §7 Course staffing

An administrator answering "who is the MCT on C3" was not on the course and did not set it up.

**One table per course:** name, role on that course, the course itself (code, dates, **full-time or part-time**), and who is supervising whom. Roles are set when a person is added to a course, which is why the same person is MCT on one and a course tutor on another. Groups come from course setup — assigned once, read everywhere.

**A tutor in training shows as supervised by name**, and the supervisor's row shows they are supervising. The relationship reads from either end.

**Two things the table surfaces that a list of names would not:** a vacancy shows in red, because a course cannot open a tutor short and that should not sit in somebody's inbox; and a strip under each table shows who is **also teaching elsewhere at the same time**, with the mode picked out — a full-time course running across a part-time one is the load nobody notices until somebody is teaching six days a week.

**The assessor appears as a separate row** at the foot of each course: whether Cambridge has appointed one, the visit date once known, and *no account — link only*.

**Connect does not hold approval paperwork.** It records what someone does on a course, not their credentials. Nobody asks a centre for approval evidence, and a system that stores it invites somebody to keep it up to date.

## §8 Leaving mid-course

Four cases. The teaching side (the letter, the CELTA 5, what carries) is drawn elsewhere; this is the administrative half — the fee, the place, the Cambridge entry, and for a deferral a promise the centre holds months later.

Every task has a **named owner**: Cambridge entry, the place, the fee, the class, the CELTA 5, the assessor pack.

### Week one — withdrawal

Entry removed before submission. The place is refillable **days one to three**; day four onwards warns (a candidate joining has missed an assessed lesson they cannot retake, so a tutor decides); week two onwards is refused. Refund per the centre's published policy.

### First half — centre deferral

**The centre's own arrangement.** Under 7.9 a candidate forced out in the first half may, at the centre's discretion, start a new course from the beginning **without paying a new fee**, carrying any assessment they passed.

- Recorded as "centre deferral from C3/2026 to C5/2026", on **both** courses. Never a bare withdrawal on one and a fresh application on the other.
- No Appian form, no Cambridge confirmation, no deferred grade. A withdrawal here and an ordinary entry there, linked.
- The course fee is waived; a second **Cambridge entry fee** is a separate decision the centre absorbs or passes on.
- Nothing carries by default — she repeats the course. Anything carried is at the centre's discretion and recorded.
- Same-mode and familiarisation rules do **not** apply: she is doing a whole course in one mode.
- Her portfolio is still in *this* course's assessor pack, however short.

### Deferral

Corrected 2026-08-27: the "more than half completed" threshold below is the superseded 2022 rule. The real June 2025 §7.9 rule has no completion threshold at all -- a deferral may be considered once **part of the course** is completed, in exceptional circumstances, at the centre's discretion, **provided the centre consults Cambridge English first**. Cambridge then separately confirms the arrangements via Appian, below -- two distinct steps, not one.

- **Appian form** submitted with the reasons and the re-integration arrangements; Cambridge confirms before anything is settled with the candidate.
- Grade on this course recorded as **deferred** — not a fail, not blank.
- She is added by name to the **new course's entry form** marked deferred, and that course's assessor is told before the visit, with comments from the previous assessor if any.
- **Six months from the end of the original course for full-time, twelve for part-time.** The window is set by the course she **left** and runs from its **end date**, not from the day she withdrew.
- **Same mode of delivery unless she agrees otherwise in writing** (7.9). If the mode changes, the centre **must provide familiarisation activities** — a build requirement, attached to the deferral.
- How much carries is the centre's judgement: 7.9 asks it to consider how much was completed and what the break will do to the outcome, and to make sure she understands the implications. Connect defaults to hours already assessed; a tutor changes it in writing.
- The place is not refilled. Fee carried, not refunded.
- **The date passing is not a silent expiry.** If the deadline arrives with nothing arranged, that is a centre failure and the app says so rather than closing the case.

**The app will not record a centre deferral past the halfway point.** Above that line it is not the centre's call.

### Final week — withdrawal

No refund under the published policy, which is why the policy must be published. A deferral is usually the better offer at that point and **must at least be discussed**, with a reason on the record if it was not offered. The assessor reads this one carefully: was she warned at Stage 1 and Stage 2, or only in week five?

## §9 Close-out and archiving

**Two things happen, and only one is an archive.**

### Exported and kept

One folder per candidate on the centre's own storage, containing:
- the portfolio — plans, feedback, self-evaluations, assignments and marks;
- **the CELTA 5 inside it**, not alongside it;
- **the grade explanation**;
- withdrawals and deferrals, however short;
- any case that was opened, with its timeline.

Connect does not hold it after the course closes.

### Not archived — duplicated

Timetable, input sessions and materials, assignment briefs and criteria, announcements and their anchors. None of it is evidence and none needs keeping. Candidate work is **never** copied forward; tutor assignments are suggested and confirmed. Duplicating a course copies its shape, never its people. The duplicate is offered **at close-out**, because the moment a centre knows what worked is the moment the course ends.

### The six months, and its reason

Final day → CELTA 5s signed. Results issued → **the clock starts** (six months runs from the issuing of results). Then, **before closing**, the grade explanation is written — once the grade is confirmed, not at the grade meeting, where grades are still provisional. Inside six months a candidate can appeal; after it, the evidence has done its job.

**The grade explanation is written for every candidate, whether they query anything or not.** It is generated from the live record, so it cannot be produced once the course has closed. Cheap while the data is there, impossible afterwards, and most people who receive one never ask another question.

**Connect is not part of the appeal.** An appeal is between the candidate, the tutor and Cambridge, months after the course has left the app. Connect's whole job is to hand the centre a folder that answers it without anybody reassembling anything.

### Close-out is refused while anything is open

An open extension, a deferral with no destination, or a live appeal all block it — and the refusal names the thing. Closing starts a six-month clock on evidence still being used.

**Naming — resolved 16 Aug, corrected against the CELTA 5 source.** The CELTA 5 booklet itself already carries two distinct, non-colliding names: **"Stage One/Two/Three Progress Record"** (the candidate tutorials) and, inside "Cambridge English Appeals Procedure," **"Appeal Stage One"** / **"Appeal Stage Two"** (the grade-challenge process). They were never actually ambiguous in Cambridge's own document — the risk was only in our casual internal shorthand ("Stage 1/2/3") collapsing both into one term. Fix: use the CELTA 5's own label verbatim — **"Progress Record — Stage 1"**, **"Progress Record — Stage 2"**, **"Progress Record — Stage 3 / Tutorial"** — everywhere in Connect's UI, and never shorten Cambridge's appeal stages below "Appeal Stage One/Two."

## §10 Documents

### Course commitments and code of conduct

A Connect template each centre edits and dates. Six sections: your time · being observed and given feedback · what you need · how we treat each other · **what the centre owes you in return** · acceptance.

Section 1 fills from the course — hours, length and the working advice all change with the mode:
- **Five weeks:** "we strongly advise against working", and Fridays are not a day off in practice.
- **Four weeks, Mon–Fri:** "you cannot work during this course" — the most compressed version, same 120 hours in a fortnight less.
- **Part-time:** designed to be taken alongside work, but two evenings and a Saturday disappear for three months.

Everything from section 2 down is identical whatever the mode.

Section 5 promises **a minimum of two progress reports** — more if needed — rather than naming tutorials, because Stage 3 is not guaranteed.

### Filming consent

Only used if a centre films. Not part of ordinary sign-up: a volunteer already consents to the short recording **they make themselves**, which is a different act.

One page: what is filmed (the camera points at the teacher; breaks are not filmed; always told beforehand) · who sees it (the trainee, their tutor, the other trainees, a Cambridge assessor, nobody else — never online, never advertising) · kept six months on the centre's storage · one consent statement carrying the right to withdraw, and a signature.

No opt-out box: they sign or they do not. Keep the signed form with the class register — **an assessor expects consent for everyone in the room**, not only the learner a trainee wrote about.

## §11 Volunteer sign-up on a laptop

The link arrives **by email**, and people open email at a desk. Same five screens, same questions, same order — never a second set of questions to maintain.

What the width buys: six languages visible without scrolling; all three consent lines on screen together (on a phone the third is below the fold, and people agree to what they can see); the six questions in two columns, answerable with a keyboard, which is the version most likely to be finished properly.

**The microphone is the one real difference.** A work laptop blocks it, mutes it, or routes it to a headset nobody is wearing. The screen tests the level before recording, says plainly if nothing is arriving, and offers to email the link so they can finish on a phone — with their answers already saved.

Still no account, at any width.
