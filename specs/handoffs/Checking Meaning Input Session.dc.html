Checking meaning — CCQs and beyond.
"Do you understand?" tells you nothing. This session is staged the way a real checking-meaning lesson is staged — writing real CCQs, spotting the bad ones, and trying the non-verbal techniques that work when questions won't.
Running this session
Ban "Do you understand?" and "Is that clear?" from the room for the next 45 minutes — including from yourself. Trainees will reach for them constantly; that's the point of banning them.
Lead-in · 2 minutes
Quick show of hands: has anyone ever said "yes, I understand" in a foreign language class, and not actually understood?

A good CCQ never contains the target language
If the question requires understanding the item to answer it, it isn't checking anything — flag this constantly, it's the single most common CCQ mistake.
Simpler language than the target, always
A CCQ pitched at or above the target item's level defeats the purpose — push trainees to go simpler than feels natural.
Non-verbal techniques aren't a fallback, they're often better
A timeline or realia can check meaning faster and more reliably than a string of questions, especially at lower levels.
