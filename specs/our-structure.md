# Our structure — what this centre does differently

A brief describing the CELTA course structure this centre runs, for anyone designing, building or reviewing the timetable. None of it changes Cambridge's requirements; all of it changes how they are met.

**Two kinds of statement appear here.** Anything unmarked was decided by the centre or comes from the Cambridge handbook and syllabus. Anything marked **[proposed]** is a design inference that has not been confirmed — it looked right when written and may not be how this centre works. Treat proposed items as questions, not decisions, and do not build from them without checking.

---

## The frame

Cambridge CELTA. 120 contact hours minimum, five days a week, 10:00 to 18:00. Two course shapes: **five weeks over 25 days** (the default) and **four weeks over 20 days** (intensive). Twelve candidates, two teaching-practice groups of six, each group splitting into subgroups of three that alternate days. Eight lessons of forty-five minutes per candidate — six hours exactly.

Nineteen input sessions of 75 minutes each, drawn from a bank of forty-seven. **[proposed]** — nineteen is a working figure; the constraint is syllabus coverage, not a count.

---

## 1 · The timetable is the source object, not a display

Every other part of the course derives from it. Move a session and its syllabus strands move, the sub-criteria it opens move, the assignment it gates moves, and the deadlines that depended on it move. Nothing is entered twice and nothing goes stale.

A reference course is published with its timetable, so duplicating a course inherits every relationship — the materials arrive already attached to their slots.

---

## 2 · Assessment is staged against the sessions

> **[proposed]** The principle is agreed. Which session teaches which sub-criterion is a design inference throughout and has never been reviewed by a tutor. Every mapping below is a starting point for that review.

The 41 criteria break into **113 sub-criteria** (Appendix 1 of the CELTA 5, Cambridge's own bullet-level wording -- corrected 2026-08-20 against the actual booklet PDF: Section 3 is 3a/3b only, no 3c). Each sub-criterion is taught by a named session and comes into scope at the first teaching practice after it. Scope only accumulates.

Several criteria are **split across sessions** because their bullets genuinely are: 4i across language analysis, concept checking and phonology; 2b split so written correction waits for the writing session. A criterion is complete only when its last bullet opens.

**The marking rule: credit above stage, never penalise above stage.** A sub-criterion whose session has not run can be marked up — as evidence toward Pass B or Pass A in the descriptor's own language — but never down. A tutor cannot write "work on your error correction" before the error-correction session, because the app will not let them.

**Scope is expressed per calendar day, never per lesson number.** Subgroups alternate, so one subgroup's lesson 4 always falls later and always has more in scope. A lesson-numbered grid would silently encode that unfairness.

**Scope freezes before the final lessons.** The last criteria open with several teaching days still to run, so the final lessons assess a stable set. No candidate meets a new criterion in their last lesson.

---

## 3 · The day has a fixed skeleton

| | |
|---|---|
| 10:00–12:15 | Teaching practice, both groups in parallel — three candidates at forty-five minutes. Non-teaching candidates peer-observe with tasks and keep the class error log. |
| 12:20–13:05 | Teaching practice feedback, group, with the tutor |
| 13:05–13:45 | Lunch |
| 13:45–15:00 | Slot A |
| 15:15–16:30 | Slot B |
| 16:45–18:00 | Supervised lesson planning, per group, tutor present. Assignment briefings and tutorial pull-outs live here. |

Teaching practice in the morning so feedback follows the lesson the same day, and so input lands on people who have just met the problem it solves. Planning last, because it prepares tomorrow using today.

---

## 4 · Development days

**Wednesdays in weeks two, three and four carry no teaching practice.** They hold the filmed observations, the tutorials, the assignment clinics and daytime writing time — none of which then competes with a lesson.

They are also the course's only slack. Six hours of assessed teaching is the Cambridge minimum and this timetable hits it exactly, so a missed lesson has to be re-taught. The development days are where that happens.

---

## 5 · Observation is in the timetable, all six hours

**Three live hours, before anyone teaches.** Day one is a demonstration lesson taught by the teaching-practice tutors to the candidates' own future learners — so it doubles as needs analysis and feeds assignment 1. Day two is an experienced teacher in an in-house class, with tasks and a debrief.

Nobody teaches a lesson shape they have never seen.

**Three filmed hours, one per development day**, each with a task and a debrief. Filmed observation is timetabled, not sent home. The handbook permits up to three hours filmed, and this uses the allowance deliberately rather than as a shortcut.

---

## 6 · A day for the Focus on the Learner assignment

> **[proposed]** The session is wanted. The reasoning given below is mine, not the centre's.

**One timetabled session where candidates work on assignment 1 together**, in the room, with a tutor present. Supervised planning time, not input — it spends none of the nineteen sessions.

It exists because assignment 1 is the first piece of written work most candidates have produced in years, it lands in week one when they are least oriented, and the failure mode is not knowing what the assignment is asking rather than not being able to do it. Half an hour together at the start prevents a fortnight of resubmissions.

---

## 7 · Assignments — staggered, owned, and gated

**Four assignments, each gated by the session that makes it answerable.** Assignment 2 cannot go out before the lexis session; assignment 3 cannot go out before the writing session. The dates are not preference — they are the earliest each becomes possible.

**Submission staggers by teaching-practice group.** Group A submits one evening, group B the next, with out-days offset by the same amount so both groups get identical windows. Fair, and the owning tutor receives six scripts rather than twelve.

**Marking is owned.** One tutor takes assignments 1 and 3, the other takes 2 and 4, each marking the whole cohort. One person holds the standard for a given assignment across all twelve.

**Deadlines are always 18:00, never morning.** Within any group, half the candidates taught the previous morning on any given day, so the only way to avoid a deadline the morning after teaching is to have no morning deadlines at all.

**[proposed] Assignment 4 carries a shorter window.** It is reflective rather than analytical — their own teaching, their own feedback, no research and no language analysis. It arrives when candidates are most tired and tutors are marking everything at once, so giving it the same window as assignment 2 wastes days the course does not have.

**Met criteria stay met.** A resubmission shows only the criteria that were not met, with round one beside it. Nothing that passed can be taken back.

**A language check runs at submission.** If a script carries enough spelling and language errors to obstruct reading, it is returned automatically with the errors marked in the candidate's own text and one hour to proofread. It never reaches a tutor, so it costs nobody their single resubmission. It cannot judge content, it skips assignment 2's analysis boxes, and it never flags quoted learner errors.

---

## 8 · Marking guidance, visible while marking

For every criterion the centre records three things: what is enough to meet it, what is **centre judgement** — the ground where two markers could legitimately differ — and what is not yet. Plus an agreed line with the date it was settled.

The middle column is the point. Nobody argues about whether a definition is wrong. They argue about verb 3 against past participle, a British model against an American one, how much detail a low level needs.

It is written from real disagreements at standardisation, sits beside the criterion while somebody marks, and is shown to tutors and the assessor but never to candidates.

---

## 9 · Tutorials are stages that produce records

Stage 1, 2 and 3, each producing something on the record rather than a conversation nobody wrote down. A minimum of two progress reports per candidate, more if needed — stage 3 is not guaranteed, so the promise is the minimum.

Candidates are never shown a trajectory. They see what they are assessed on this week and what is coming, with dates. Whether they are heading for a Pass A is a tutorial with a person.

---

## 10 · Learners are volunteers we recruit

Volunteer learners sign up through a public page in their own language — eight questions, consent, and one short recording in English so a level can be judged. No account and no password. They are emailed when a class needs them.

The register records who signed up against who attended, and travels into the assessor pack.

---

## Decisions from the course walkthrough

**"I don't know which course yet" is a valid answer on the application form.** Applicants who pick it sit in a **general pool** rather than any course's pipeline, and the interviewer settles the intake during the interview. This also handles the applicant who applied for one course but is clearly better suited to another — the conversation decides, not the form.

**Every candidate does the getting-to-know-you teach on day one.** It was going to be ABC on day one and DEF on day two. All six on day one is fairer: split across two days, the second group meets its learners a day later and teaches TP1 with less contact. Day one carries no assessed teaching practice, so there is room.

## The candidate's name is captured once, at application

Two fields on the application form, not one.

- **Name as it appears on your passport** — with the warning that this is what will be printed on the Cambridge certificate. It flows into the CELTA 5, the entry form and the certificate without anyone retyping it, which removes the commonest cause of a reissued certificate.
- **What you would like to be called** — for the register, the course page and everything day to day.

Nothing is handwritten on the CELTA 5 cover on day one. The tutor shows the candidate their name already there.

## The course introduction

The first block of day one. Administrative rather than teaching, so it sits before U01 on the timetable rather than being part of it.

It covers the timetable, the shape of the course, the CELTA 5 and the portfolio — and about ten minutes on Connect: where the timetable is, where the portfolio is, where feedback appears, where things are handed in. Nobody learns software from being shown it, so the treasure hunt does the real work.

Two things it also does: a light check on the pre-course task, which tells a tutor in hour one who arrived cold; and setting up the unassessed getting-to-know-you teach, which every candidate does on day one.

**And one forward reference: assignment 1.** Candidates are told before they meet the learners that they will need one of them for Focus on the Learner — so pay attention today, and take notes. The assignment goes out on day one or two, and the learner they choose is one they met in the getting-to-know-you teach. Without the warning, they spend that teach unobserving.

## Before the course starts

The order everything happens in, which runs the opposite way from how a course feels.

1. **The course is created as a shell** as soon as its dates are decided — code, dates, mode, places. Months ahead, not weeks. Everything else hangs off it.
2. **It appears on the public application form** as an intake candidates can choose. Applicants who have not decided pick "I don't know which course yet" and sit in a general pool until the interview settles it.
3. **Admissions runs against it for months** — applications, tasks, triage, interviews, offers, deposits. This is why a course cannot be created a fortnight before it starts.
4. **Tutors are named when they are appointed**, not on a schedule, and see the course from that moment — empty at first, filling as the timetable is built.
5. **The teaching setup happens about a fortnight out** — timetable, sessions, TP points, groups, materials. Or, for any course after the first, it arrives by duplicating the reference course.
6. **The workspace invitation goes when the deposit clears** — automatically through a connected provider, or marked by an administrator when the centre has agreed terms.
7. **The Friday before**, one email: the course starts Monday, sign in if you have not, do the treasure hunt and the three activities.

Nothing about the teaching side is visible to candidates before they are invited, and nothing about admissions is visible to tutors.

## Observation is a card, like teaching practice

Every observation — live or filmed — is a card on its timetable slot, carrying:

- the **lesson plan** the tutor taught from (live demos only)
- the **materials** used
- the **observation task**, completed by each candidate

The completed task goes into the candidate's portfolio the way a self-evaluation does. So six hours of directed observation is six cards and six completed tasks per candidate, and when the assessor asks for evidence of 10.1 it assembles itself.

This is what makes it *directed* rather than watching, which is the handbook's own word. **A demo or filmed observation cannot be timetabled without a task attached** — the same rule as a teaching practice slot without a TP point.

**The tutor writes a self-evaluation on their own demo**, on the same form the candidates will use, and it goes on the card. Candidates read it before writing their first one. It teaches reflection by showing it — including what the tutor thought did not work — and it settles the fear that admitting a weakness costs you something. Nobody learns self-evaluation from a session about self-evaluation.

## Questions for the reader

What else does this centre do that a visiting tutor would find unfamiliar? The list above is what has been designed so far. Anything not on it either matches ordinary practice or has not been decided yet — and the difference matters.

## What still needs a tutor's eye

- The session-to-sub-criterion mapping in full. It is the core of the assessment model and none of it has been confirmed.
- The nineteen sessions: their content, their order, and whether nineteen is the right number.
- Which assignments genuinely need shorter or longer windows, and why.
- The syllabus strand titles, which follow the published syllabus rather than the centre's own copy.
